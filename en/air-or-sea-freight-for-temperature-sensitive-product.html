Should temperature-sensitive medicines ship by air or by sea?

By LNJC· 17 October 2026· 6 min read

The section of WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, that covers air and sea transport does not rank one mode above the other. Whichever is used, the carrier must work under a formal service level agreement, and the packaging must be qualified for the specific route and the ambient temperatures expected from departure to arrival.

Does WHO guidance say whether to ship by air or by sea?

No. It sets the same conditions for both. WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, Model guidance for the storage and transport of time- and temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical products (2011), deals with the two modes in a single section, 6.4.1 Air and sea transport. It does not compare cost or transit time.1

What it does say is this: ensure that any carrier contracted to transport time- and temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical products (TTSPPs) by air or by sea "operates under the terms of a formal service level agreement (SLA) drawn up between the parties". The carrier "is to be made responsible for maintaining load temperatures within the transport temperature profile defined for each product".1

What should the agreement with the carrier cover?

At minimum, the carrier's responsibility for load temperature. That is the one obligation section 6.4.1 names: the carrier is made responsible for maintaining load temperatures within the transport temperature profile defined for each product.1 Each product therefore needs a defined transport temperature profile before it ships.

Beyond that, common industry practice is to agree how the load is monitored, who is told when a reading goes out of range, and how records are handed over. Those details are practice, not part of the WHO text. For what should happen when a deviation does occur, see temperature excursions in transit and the evidence to expect.

What must the shipping container do?

Protect the product and people, and show it was not tampered with. Section 6.8.1 of Annex 9 asks shippers to select shipping containers that:1

  • comply with applicable national and international standards relevant to the product type and the chosen transport route and mode or modes;
  • protect personnel and the general public from hazards arising from spillage, leakage or excessive internal pressure;
  • protect the product against mechanical damage and the anticipated ambient temperature range that will be encountered in transit; and
  • can be closed in a manner that allows the recipient of the consignment to establish that the product has not been tampered with during transport.

How is packaging qualified for a particular route?

By showing it holds the product within its stability range over that route and duration. Annex 9 covers two kinds of container.1

Insulated passive containers (section 6.8.3) are to be qualified together with all necessary ancillary packaging. The guidance lists examples including dry ice, ice or gel packs, phase change materials and dunnage. The qualified system must be capable of maintaining the product within the temperature range needed to meet its stability profile as stated by the manufacturer. Qualification should include full details of the packaging assembly, the thermal conditioning regime, and the minimum and maximum shipping volume, weight and thermal mass the container can safely accommodate, as well as the correct placement of temperature monitors where these are used.1

Active containers (section 6.8.4) must likewise be shown capable of maintaining the product within the temperature range needed to meet the stability profile stated by the manufacturer.1

For both, the guidance adds the same requirement: take account of the transport route and of the anticipated ambient temperature profile over the duration of transport, "measured from the point of departure to the point of arrival in the recipient's temperature-controlled store".1 The journey ends inside the receiving store, not at the port or airport.

So how do you choose between air and sea?

Start from the product and the route, not from the mode. Three inputs drive the decision:

  • The product's stability profile. The manufacturer states the temperature range the product needs; packaging is qualified against it. Our guide to stability data for hot, humid markets covers where that profile comes from.
  • The route. Sections 6.8.1, 6.8.3 and 6.8.4 all tie the container to the route.
  • The duration, door to store. The qualified packaging must cover the whole time from departure to the recipient's store.

In practice, a shipper tests each realistic option, by air or by sea, against those inputs and asks whether qualified packaging and a carrier under a suitable agreement exist for it. A longer journey is not ruled out, but the packaging must be qualified for that duration. Transit times and costs depend on the specific lane and carrier, so no figure is offered here.

How does this apply to shipments for the Republic of Yemen?

The same principle applies: choose mode and packaging for the actual route, including the final leg to the receiving store. Any local requirements for a particular consignment are confirmed case by case, and are worth raising before the first shipment is planned.

Frequently asked questions

Should temperature-sensitive medicines ship by air or by sea?

WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, does not rank one mode above the other in its section on air and sea transport. It asks that any air or sea carrier works under a formal service level agreement and that the packaging is qualified for the route and the ambient temperatures expected over the whole journey. The right mode follows from the route, the duration and the product's stability profile.

What does WHO guidance require of an air or sea carrier?

Section 6.4.1 of WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, says any carrier contracted to transport time- and temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical products by air or by sea should operate under a formal service level agreement between the parties. The carrier is to be made responsible for maintaining load temperatures within the transport temperature profile defined for each product.

How are passive and active shipping containers qualified?

WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, treats them as two kinds of container. Insulated passive containers are qualified together with their ancillary packaging, such as ice or gel packs, dry ice or phase change materials. Active containers are qualified on their own. Both must be shown to hold the product within its stability profile over the transport route.

Where does the transport temperature profile end?

Not at the port or the airport. Sections 6.8.3 and 6.8.4 of WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9, measure the anticipated ambient temperature profile from the point of departure to the point of arrival in the recipient's temperature-controlled store, so container qualification must cover the whole journey to that store.
