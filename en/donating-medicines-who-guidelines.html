Donating medicines to a market like Yemen: what the WHO guidelines ask of the donor

By LNJC· 31 October 2026· 6 min read

The WHO Guidelines for Medicine Donations, Revised 2010, ask a donor to give only against the recipient's expressed need, in agreed quantities, from a quality-ensured source, with at least one year of shelf life remaining on arrival, labelled in a language health professionals understand, and with transport and clearance costs paid by the donor unless agreed otherwise.

What are the WHO guidelines for medicine donations?

They are guidance on good donation practice, published by the World Health Organization with partner agencies as Guidelines for Medicine Donations, Revised 2010.1 They describe what a useful donation to a high-need market such as the Republic of Yemen looks like.

The document is explicit about its own status. It says: "These guidelines are not an international regulatory document." It is intended to achieve best donation practice by donors and recipients, and to serve as a basis for national or institutional donation guidelines.1 Read it as best practice, not law.

What are the four core principles?

The WHO guidelines rest on four principles. First, donations should benefit the recipient to the maximum extent possible, should be based on an expressed need, and unsolicited donations are to be discouraged. Second, donations should respect the wishes and authority of the recipient and conform with the government policies and administrative arrangements of the recipient country.1

Third, there should be effective coordination and collaboration between donor and recipient, with every donation made according to a plan formulated by both parties. Fourth, there should be no double standard in quality: in the guidelines' words, "If the quality of an item is unacceptable in the donor country, it is also unacceptable as a donation."1

Who decides what and how much to donate?

The recipient does. Guideline 1 says all medicine donations should be based on an expressed need, should be relevant to the disease pattern in the recipient country, and that quantities should be agreed between donor and recipient.1

The explanation puts the prime responsibility for specifying needs, and for determining quantities, with the recipient. It warns that unsolicited or unneeded donations may lead to over-stocking and expiry.1 The first step is a conversation with the recipient, not a shipment.

What quality standard should donated medicines meet?

The same standard you would accept at home. Guideline 4 says donated medicines should be obtained from a quality-ensured source and should comply with quality standards in both donor and recipient countries, and that the WHO Certification Scheme on the Quality of Pharmaceutical Products Moving in International Commerce should be used.1 For how that scheme works in practice, see what a certificate of a pharmaceutical product is.

The explanation adds that donated medicines should be authorised for sale in the country of origin and manufactured in accordance with international standards of good manufacturing practices (GMP).1

Guideline 5 rules out two sources entirely: medicines that have been issued to patients and then returned to a pharmacy or elsewhere, and medicines given to health professionals as free samples.1 The same caution about returned stock runs through good distribution practice; see returns from a distant market.

How much remaining shelf life should donated medicines have?

At least one year after arrival. Guideline 6 says all donated medicines should have a remaining shelf life of at least one year after arrival in the recipient country, and that donated quantities should match what can be consumed before expiry, based on national consumption and the quantities already in stock or in the supply pipeline.1

Distribution through the storage levels may take a number of months, and the guidelines call the argument that short-dated products suit acute emergencies "incorrect", because in emergencies donated medicines tend to accumulate.1

Two exceptions are allowed. One is for direct donations to specific health facilities, where professional staff know the shelf life is limited and the quantity and remaining shelf life allow use before expiry. The other covers medicines with a total shelf life of less than two years: at least one third of the shelf life should remain, with a minimum of six months. The guidelines add that national donation policy may specify its own minimum.1 For the commercial equivalent, see remaining shelf life at shipment.

How should donated medicines be labelled?

In a language the recipient's health professionals easily understand. Guideline 7 says the label on each container should carry at least the International Nonproprietary Name (INN) or generic name, batch number, dosage form, strength, name of manufacturer, country of manufacture, quantity in the container, storage conditions and expiry date.1

Who pays for donated medicines to reach patients?

The donor, unless the recipient agrees otherwise in advance. Guideline 12 says the costs of international and local transport, warehousing, port clearance and customs storage, handling, and disposal or reverse logistics of expired donated products should be paid for by the donor agency, unless specifically agreed otherwise with the recipient in advance.1

The stated purpose is to stop recipients being forced to spend effort and money on unannounced or unwanted donations.1 Budget for these costs from the outset.

How does this apply to donations to the Republic of Yemen?

The WHO guidelines are an international reference, not a statement of what the Republic of Yemen requires or permits for donated medicines. The guidelines themselves point to the recipient country's own government policies and national donation policy, and local specifics are confirmed case by case with the recipient and the relevant authorities. For the wider market picture, see entering the Yemen market.

Frequently asked questions

What do the WHO guidelines for medicine donations ask of a donor?

The WHO Guidelines for Medicine Donations, Revised 2010, ask a donor to give only against an expressed need, in quantities agreed with the recipient, from a quality-ensured source authorised for sale in the country of origin, with at least one year of shelf life on arrival, labelled in an understood language, and with transport and clearance costs paid unless agreed otherwise.

Are the WHO guidelines for medicine donations legally binding?

No. The guidelines say of themselves that they are not an international regulatory document. They are guidance intended to achieve best donation practice by donors and recipients, and a basis for national or institutional donation guidelines. What a recipient country requires is set by its own national policy.

How much remaining shelf life should donated medicines have?

Guideline 6 of the WHO Guidelines for Medicine Donations says donated medicines should have a remaining shelf life of at least one year after arrival in the recipient country. For medicines with a total shelf life under two years, at least one third should remain, with a minimum of six months. National donation policy may set its own minimum.

Who pays the costs of a medicine donation?

Guideline 12 of the WHO Guidelines for Medicine Donations says the donor agency should pay for international and local transport, warehousing, port clearance, customs storage, handling, and disposal or reverse logistics of expired donated products, unless the recipient has specifically agreed otherwise in advance.
